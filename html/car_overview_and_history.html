Formula 1 Cars Research Brief

Purpose: Reference material for an interactive Three.js website about Formula 1 cars, aimed at a general audience (readers who have never watched F1).

Research date: 14 October 2026 (Asia/Kuala_Lumpur, MYT).

Units: Metric first; imperial in brackets where helpful.

Method: Figures taken from FIA technical materials, Formula1.com, manufacturer/team sites, Pirelli, and selected technical press. Every numeric claim has a source link. Conflicting or soft figures are marked UNVERIFIED.

Part 1 — Car overview (2026 regulations)

What is a Formula 1 car?

A Formula 1 (F1) car is a single-seat racing car built to a strict rule book written by the FIA (Fédération Internationale de l’Automobile — the sport’s governing body). Teams design their own chassis (the main body and structure), while a small number of manufacturers supply the power unit (the engine plus electric hybrid system). Tyres come from one supplier, Pirelli. The cars race on purpose-built circuits and some street courses around the world.

Main visual differences vs 2022–2025 cars

  1. Smaller and lighter. Shorter wheelbase (−200 mm), narrower body (−100 mm), slimmer tyres, lower minimum weight. The “nimble car” idea is to make cars easier to place in corners and leave more track space for side-by-side racing (FIA; F1.com).
  1. Active aerodynamics instead of DRS-only. Both front and rear wings have moveable flaps. In Corner Mode (also called Z-mode in earlier FIA wording) flaps give high downforce (air pushing the car into the track for grip). In Straight Mode (X-mode) flaps open like a Venetian blind to cut drag (air resistance) so the car goes faster in a straight line. Unlike old DRS (Drag Reduction System — a rear-wing flap only the chasing car could open when close enough), Straight Mode is available to every car in designated zones (FIA; F1.com aero).
  1. Flatter floor; Venturi tunnels gone. The deep underfloor tunnels of 2022–2025 (a return of “ground effect”) are removed. The floor is flatter with a larger diffuser (the upswept rear underbody that slows and expands airflow). Ground-effect suction is much weaker (F1.com).
  1. Simpler rear wing, no beam wing. The small wing under the main rear wing (beam wing) is banned; endplates are simpler; front wing is narrower with a two-element active flap (FIA).
  1. Wheel covers removed; bargeboards return. Front wheel arches used in the previous generation are gone. Small bargeboards (vertical panels) help pull dirty air from the front wheels inward so the car behind has cleaner air (F1.com).
  1. Power unit looks similar but is electrically much stronger. Still a 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid, but without the MGU-H and with roughly a 50/50 combustion/electric power split (see Part 4).

Fun fact: The FIA says the 2026 rules cut downforce by about 30% and drag by about 55% versus the previous concept — less “stuck to the road,” more efficient in a straight line (FIA).

Part 2 — Exploded view components

Each entry: plain-language name, where it sits, what it does, materials, approx size/weight, fun fact. Sizes/weights that are team-secret or era-dependent are marked UNVERIFIED.

Survival cell / monocoque

Monocoque means the outer shell is also the structure — like an eggshell that is both container and strength. In F1 it is the survival cell: the carbon-fibre “tub” that surrounds the driver from the pedals to behind the seat.

It sits in the middle of the car; the nose, sidepods, floor, engine and gearbox bolt to it.

Modern cells use carbon-fibre composite skins with honeycomb cores (often aluminium honeycomb). Approximate mass of the bare tub is often quoted around 30–40 kg, but exact figures are team-confidential — UNVERIFIED for 2026.

Lotus introduced a stressed aluminium monocoque (Lotus 25, 1962); McLaren’s MP4/1 (1981) popularised full carbon-fibre cells (The Engineer F1@75).

Fun fact: Crash tests get tougher each rules cycle — 2026 raises roll-hoop loads and adds a two-stage nose impact structure (FIA).

Front wing

A multi-element wing hanging under the nose. It creates downforce at the front and carefully steers airflow around the front tyres toward the floor and sidepods.

2026: narrower (−100 mm), simpler, with a two-element active flap that drops in Straight Mode (FIA).

Materials: carbon-fibre composite. Width approaches the front track but is regulated; weight is a few kilograms as a complete assembly — UNVERIFIED exact kg.

Fun fact: Teams used to adjust front-wing angle only in the garage or pit stop; now the car can change flap angle on the move in legal zones (F1.com).

Rear wing & active aero (replacing DRS)

The tall wing at the back. In Corner Mode it makes strong rear downforce; in Straight Mode its top elements open to cut drag.

2026 uses a three-element active rear wing; the old beam wing underneath is gone (FIA).

How this replaces DRS: From 2011–2025, DRS let only a chasing car (usually within 1 second) open a rear flap in special zones. Active aero Straight Mode is available to all cars in designated straight zones; a separate Overtake Mode (extra battery energy when within ~1 second at a detection point) takes over the “help the car behind” role (F1.com; F1.com PU).

Materials: carbon fibre; actuators and sensors inside the endplates/wing body.

Fun fact: Think of Straight Mode as the car temporarily “closing its parachute” so it can sprint down the straight, then opening the parachute again for the next corner.

Floor & Venturi tunnels

The underside of the car. In 2022–2025, deep Venturi tunnels sped up air under the car so pressure dropped and the car was sucked down (ground effect — using the road surface like the other half of a wing).

In 2026 those tunnels are removed; the floor is flatter with a larger rear diffuser. Some underbody performance remains, but it is less dominant (F1.com).

A sacrificial plank under the floor wears if the car scrapes; too much wear fails scrutineering.

Materials: carbon sandwich; plank often wood/composite per regulations. Overall floor is roughly car-width × wheelbase-scale — largest aero part by area.

Fun fact: Ground effect was invented for F1 by Lotus in the late 1970s, banned in the 1980s, brought back in 2022, then dialled back again for 2026 (F1.com history).

Sidepods & cooling

The bodywork beside the cockpit. They house radiators and coolers for the engine, turbo, oil, and hybrid electronics, and shape airflow toward the rear wing and floor.

Intakes scoop air; outlets dump hot air. Packaging is a big performance differentiator between teams.

Materials: carbon bodywork over metal heat exchangers. Size: roughly the volume of two large suitcases split left/right — UNVERIFIED exact litres.

Fun fact: Sidepod shapes became famous in 2022 (including Mercedes’ extreme “zero sidepod” look); 2026 rules again reshape what teams are allowed to do with wheel wake and in-wash boards (FIA).

Power unit (ICE, MGU-K, energy store, control electronics)

Power unit (PU) = everything that makes propulsion power, not just the petrol engine.

  • ICE — Internal Combustion Engine: the 1.6-litre turbocharged V6 that burns fuel.
  • MGU-K — Motor Generator Unit–Kinetic: an electric motor/generator on the drivetrain that recovers energy when slowing and can drive the wheels when boosting.
  • Energy store (ES) — the battery pack that holds recovered electricity.
  • Control electronics (CE) — the computers and power electronics that manage hybrid energy.
  • (2014–2025 also had an MGU-H harvesting turbo/exhaust heat; removed for 2026.)

Location: behind the driver, ahead of the gearbox, with radiators in the sidepods and the battery typically low in the chassis.

2026 headline numbers: ICE ≈ 400 kW, MGU-K 350 kW, target ≈ 50/50 split, total peak still 700+ kW class (FIA; Mercedes; F1.com).

Previous-generation energy store minimum mass was 20 kg (Mercedes W15/W16 tech sheets) — treat 2026 battery mass as UNVERIFIED until a 2026 homologation figure is published for your site.

Fun fact: Removing the complex MGU-H helped attract new manufacturers (Audi, Red Bull Ford, Honda return as full works) (F1.com).

Gearbox

Sits behind the engine and drives the rear wheels. Modern F1 uses a semi-automatic sequential gearbox: the driver pulls paddles on the steering wheel; electro-hydraulic actuators change gears in milliseconds without a traditional H-pattern stick. Eight forward gears + reverse have been standard in the hybrid era (F1.com regulation history context; industry explainers).

The case is often carbon and/or titanium/aluminium; it also acts as a stressed member carrying rear suspension. Complete gearbox mass is typically tens of kilograms — historic carbon cases were reported far lighter than magnesium; treat modern total ≈ under ~40 kg as UNVERIFIED order-of-magnitude (historical technical press).

Fun fact: Ferrari’s 640 (1989) brought paddle-shift semi-auto gearboxes to F1; every car uses the idea today (The Engineer).

Suspension (push-rod / pull-rod)

Connects wheels to the chassis so the car can absorb bumps while keeping the aero platform stable.

Wishbones (A-shaped arms) locate each wheel. A push-rod is a rod that is compressed when the wheel rises, pushing on an inboard rocker that works the spring/damper. A pull-rod is tugged when the wheel rises, pulling a lower rocker. Neither is “always better” — teams pick based on aero packaging (F1.com explainer; The Race).

Materials: carbon wishbones, steel/titanium rods, inboard torsion bars and dampers.

Fun fact: In the early 1990s, active suspension (computers constantly adjusting ride height) made Williams almost unbeatable — then it was banned (F1.com).

Brakes (carbon-carbon, brake-by-wire)

F1 uses carbon-carbon discs and pads: carbon fibres in a carbon matrix. They work best when very hot (hundreds of °C) and are much lighter than road-car iron discs. A full corner assembly is only on the order of a few kilograms — UNVERIFIED exact 2026 masses (Racecar Engineering / Brembo technical explainers).

Brake-by-wire on the rear: when the MGU-K harvests energy under braking, computers blend regenerative braking with the hydraulic rear brakes so the driver still feels a predictable pedal (Autosport). Front brakes remain primarily hydraulic.

Fun fact: Carbon brakes can exceed 1,000 °C — glowing discs in night races are real, not camera effects (Brembo).

Tyres (slicks, intermediates, full wets)

Pirelli supplies every team. Dry tyres are slicks — smooth tread for maximum contact on dry asphalt. Intermediates (green sidewall marking) have shallow grooves for light rain. Full wets (blue) have deep grooves for heavy rain. Dry compounds are labelled Hard (white), Medium (yellow), Soft (red) from a C1–C5 range (Pirelli; Mercedes race preview).

2026 sizes: fronts 280×705 mm, rears 375×710 mm on 18-inch rims (see Part 1). Four tyres ≈ 46 kg estimated in the FIA minimum-weight split (FIA).

Fun fact: Slicks returned in 2009 after a decade of grooved dry tyres that had been introduced to slow the cars (F1.com).

Halo

A titanium “halo” bar above the cockpit that protects the driver’s head from debris and from other cars.

Structure: Grade 5 titanium (Ti-6Al-4V), about 7 kg, covered in carbon fairings for aero. Mandatory in F1 since 2018. Tests include large static loads (order of 125 kN in homologation tests described by manufacturers/FIA coverage) (Racecar Engineering; FIA safety legacy).

Fun fact: Drivers once disliked how it looked; it has since been credited with protecting drivers in several serious accidents (Spa 2018, Bahrain 2020, Silverstone 2022 among widely cited cases) (Racecar Engineering; FIA).

Steering wheel (main controls)

A detachable, roughly rectangular controller packed with buttons, rotary dials, paddles and a small screen. It is not like a road-car wheel — it is closer to an aircraft sidestick’s button layout.

Typical controls (labels vary by team; 2026 renames some hybrid modes):

  • Paddles: gear up/down; clutch paddle(s) for race starts.
  • Pit limiter: caps speed in the pit lane.
  • Radio / drink / differential / brake-balance dials.
  • ERS / Boost: deploy battery power (2026 “Boost”).
  • Overtake / Straight Mode related controls as mapped for 2026 (Straight Mode is often automatic in zones; Overtake Mode is proximity-based) (F1.com PU article).
  • Older wheels had a dedicated DRS button; that system is gone for 2026.

Materials: carbon shell, magnesium/aluminium internals; mass roughly 1–2 kg class — UNVERIFIED exact.

Fun fact: Drivers can swap a spare wheel in seconds if theirs fails — the whole unit unplugs from a quick-release hub.

Milestone highlights (for then-vs-now UI)

  1. Mid-engine layout (late 1950s) — Engine moved behind the driver (Cooper). Better balance; every F1 car since follows this.
  2. Wings (1968) — Lotus and others add inverted aerofoils for downforce.
  3. Ground effect 1970s–early 1980s — Lotus 78/79 tunnels; banned via flat bottoms (1983).
  4. Turbo era (1977–1988) — Renault pioneers; qualifying boost reaches extreme power.
  5. Active suspension (early 1990s) — Williams FW14B; banned 1994.
  6. Semi-auto gearboxes (1989 onward) — Ferrari 640 paddles.
  7. Hybrid era (KERS 2009; full PU 2014–2025) — Electric boost becomes central.
  8. Halo (2018) — Titanium head protection.
  9. Ground effect return (2022–2025) then 2026 reset — tunnels out; active aero and bigger electric share in.

Safety incidents and rule changes (factual, respectful)

Racing is dangerous; the sport’s modern record is far safer because of lessons paid for by earlier generations. Summaries below avoid graphic detail.

  • Imola 1994 (San Marino GP): Weekend tragedies involving Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna shocked the sport. The FIA accelerated circuit changes, car performance cuts (including floor plank), cockpit protection, and a long-term safety research culture (FIA).
  • 1990s–2000s incremental rules: Higher cockpit sides, wheel tethers (from 1999, later multi-tether), extractable seats, stricter helmets, HANS head-and-neck support mandatory in F1 from 2003 (FIA).
  • Felipe Massa (Hungary 2009) and related debris risks: Led to stronger helmet/visor protection (Zylon panel era) (FIA).
  • Jules Bianchi (Japan 2014): Ultimately fatal injuries after a wet-weather accident involving a recovery vehicle. The Virtual Safety Car (2015) and further cockpit intrusion work followed; closed-cockpit research contributed to the Halo path (FIA).
  • Halo era incidents (2018 onward): Multiple crashes where the Halo was credited with reducing head injury risk (e.g. Spa 2018, Bahrain 2020, Silverstone 2022). Grosjean’s 2020 Bahrain barrier crash also triggered a broad safety review of barriers, clothing, and rescue (FIA; Racecar Engineering).
  • 2026: Stronger roll-hoop test (16 g → 20 g), two-stage front impact structure, increased side intrusion protection (FIA).

Energy recovery — plain-language steps (for an animated diagram)

What “hybrid” means here: The car has a petrol engine and an electric motor that can charge a battery and also push the car.

  1. Driver brakes or lifts. The car must lose speed; that energy used to become heat in the brakes.
  2. MGU-K acts as a generator. Instead of wasting all that energy, the MGU-K turns motion into electricity (regenerative braking), while brake-by-wire keeps the pedal feel consistent.
  3. Electricity flows into the energy store. The battery (energy store) fills up within rules limits for the lap.
  4. Extra harvesting tricks (2026). Systems can also harvest on part-throttle, when the driver lifts early (“lift and coast”), or via limited “super clipping” near the end of a straight (F1.com).
  5. Driver (or map) deploys Boost. The MGU-K switches to motor mode and adds up to 350 kW at the drivetrain — like a temporary second engine (Mercedes).
  6. Overtake Mode (if eligible). When close enough at the detection point, the following car may get +0.5 MJ and a stronger deployment profile next lap — the sporting replacement for old DRS assistance (F1.com).
  7. What changed vs 2014–2025. The old MGU-H also made electricity from turbo/exhaust heat. It is gone; almost all electrical strategy now centres on the bigger MGU-K and battery (FIA).

Everyday comparison: Like a city bus that charges when slowing for a stop, then uses that charge to pull away — except an F1 MGU-K is many times more powerful and the strategy is fought over every corner.

2026 fuel in one paragraph

Cars must run advanced sustainable fuel: molecules from sources such as non-food biomass, municipal waste, or sustainable carbon capture — certified so no new fossil carbon enters the fuel. It is designed as a drop-in petrol substitute (works in a petrol engine without a special new combustion architecture) (FIA; F1.com).
